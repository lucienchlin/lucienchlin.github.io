用了源頭產製於中國的 AI 模組，就一定會直接損害臺灣的主權文化或相應自主性嗎？

會，或是說有機會，這個可能性不能簡單被排除；但也不能被簡化為一定會，或一定會產生立即的影響。畢竟，我們也有像翁曉玲女士這樣自述「身在台灣、心在中國」的立法委員（委員自述、非中傷），而臺灣的主權運作與自由法制也沒有立刻就倒。（也不是說一定不會倒、但……下略 3000 字……）

所以呢？就用嗎？直接用嗎？都不設標準或防線了？就用！？

也不是。既然這些「潛意識」是用塞的，那麼對「中國式」AI 模組的使用，原則上不會有立即如毒蛇猛獸般的影響，但可能會有長效潛移默化的影響。所以，就像食安法要求食品成分與有害物質檢測必須被透明揭露一般，我們未來透過 AI 餵給兒童、學子、老人家的「精神食糧」，也應該要被透明揭露。

進一步說，部分網友提出靠「原產地」來禁中國 AI 模組，或認為此作法已不切實際，其論點有其立場。畢竟這些 AI 模組採 Open-Weight 模式向全球發布後，會再經轉化。例如新加坡國家級的 SEA-LION 計畫，明確採用中國 Alibaba 的 Qwen 開放權重模型作為重要基礎模型，再以新加坡及東南亞資料進行持續預訓練（continued pre-training）、fine-tuning / post-training 等調校。經過調校之後，還算是中國模型或中國原生模型嗎？

也就是說，採原產地作為評判標準，這些模型經過第三地機構調校時，捉得緊時，會阻絕臺灣近用進步模型的機會；捉得鬆時，洗產地變得非常容易，也不會得到管制上的實效。

所以我想，評測基礎應該從「OSAID－開源 AI 定義」裡取經。在 OSAID 裡，並沒有要求開源 AI 模組的訓練資料一定要提供給後手，但必須明確羅列訓練資料的相關資訊（Data Information）。有了這些 Data Information，一來相關 AI 模型比較容易被比對是不是「塞了不應該存在的東西」，也可以按圖索驥，另以其他客觀的訓練資料進行調校替換。

所以說，個人支持 General-Purpose AI Model（GPAI model，通用人工智慧模型／通用 AI 模型）若無特別例外理由，應一致性符合 OSAID 的定義標準。因為 GPAI model 具有顯著通用性、能勝任廣泛不同任務，並可整合進各種下游系統或應用，涉及社會多數公眾的共通價值與利益，就是需要這樣的開誠布公。

就像 Vue.js 由中國出生的開發者尤雨溪（Evan You）於 2014 年建立，然而因採 MIT 授權發布，所有程式源碼的開發及其歷程均公開可及（Publicly Available）；於 2016 年成立、支持 Vue.js 開發、維護及其周邊商業活動的 Vue Technology LLC 也設立於美國。故行政院 109 年 12 月 25 日國家資通安全會報第 36 次委員會議決定，不認為 Vue.js 因主要開發者為中國國籍就帶有資安疑慮，將其解除列管，並不完全禁止於公共採購之列。

與此相應的，日本政府於 2026 年 8 月 18 日召開的「AI 時代智慧財產權檢討會」，所提出的草案內容，也要求 AI 業者應廣泛公開 AI 模組產製過程、涉及的學習資料及資料蒐集方式。

也就是說，未來若無法明確交代 AI 產製與訓練過程，以及相關資料列表的 AI 方案，原則上就不應進入公共採購或一般性廣域應用的範疇，以此透明課責性與權利義務的平衡，降低 AI 廣泛應用所可能帶來的風險。

Does using an AI model originally developed in China necessarily and directly undermine Taiwan’s sovereign culture or corresponding autonomy?

It can—or, more precisely, there is a possibility that it could. That possibility cannot simply be ruled out. But neither should it be reduced to the claim that it necessarily will, or that any impact will necessarily be immediate. After all, we even have legislators such as Ms Weng Hsiao-ling, who has described herself as “physically in Taiwan, but with my heart in China” (her own words, not a smear), and Taiwan’s sovereign governance and liberal legal order have not immediately collapsed as a result. So what then? Just use them? Use them outright? Set no standards or safeguards whatsoever and simply go ahead!?

Not quite. If these “subconscious” elements are being embedded into the models, then the use of “Chinese-style” AI models will not, as a rule, produce an immediate threat akin to some venomous beast, but it may exert subtle, long-term influence. Just as food safety laws require transparent disclosure of food ingredients and testing for harmful substances, the “food for thought” that we will increasingly feed through AI to children, students and elder people should likewise be transparently disclosed.

Taking this a step further, some commenters have proposed banning Chinese AI models based on their “country of origin”, while others argue that such an approach is already impractical. There is a case to be made for the latter view. Once these AI models are released globally on an open-weight basis, they can subsequently be transformed. Singapore’s national-level SEA-LION programme, for example, explicitly uses Alibaba’s Chinese Qwen open-weight model as an important foundation model, and then further adapts it using Singaporean and Southeast Asian data through continued pre-training, fine-tuning, post-training and other techniques. After such adaptation, should it still be regarded as a Chinese model, or a model of Chinese origin?

In other words, using country of origin as the criterion for assessment creates a dilemma once these models are further adapted by institutions in third countries. If the rule is drawn too tightly, Taiwan may be denied opportunities to access advanced models; if it is drawn too loosely, “origin laundering” becomes extremely easy, without producing meaningful regulatory results.

I therefore think that our assessment framework should take a lesson from the OSAID—Open Source AI Definition. OSAID does not require the training data of an open-source AI model to be made available to downstream recipients, but it does require relevant information about the training data (Data Information) to be clearly provided. With such Data Information, it becomes easier to examine whether an AI model has been “stuffed with things that should not be there”. It also allows us to trace the sources and, where appropriate, use other objective training data to further adapt or replace problematic elements.

Accordingly, I support requiring General-Purpose AI Models (GPAI models), absent specific reasons for an exception, to consistently meet the standards of the OSAID definition. GPAI models possess significant generality, are capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks, and can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems and applications. Because they implicate common values and interests shared by the broader public, this is precisely where openness and transparency are needed.

Consider Vue.js. It was created in 2014 by Evan You (尤雨溪), a developer born in China. Yet it was released under the MIT License, with all of its source code development and development history being publicly available. Vue Technology LLC, established in 2016 to support the development and maintenance of Vue.js and its surrounding commercial activities, was also established in the United States. Accordingly, at the 36th meeting of Taiwan’s National Information and Communication Security Taskforce on 25 December 2020, the Executive Yuan determined that Vue.js should not be regarded as posing cybersecurity concerns merely because its principal developer was a Chinese national. It was therefore removed from the controlled list rather than being categorically prohibited from public procurement.

In a related development, a draft presented at the Japanese government’s “Study Group on Intellectual Property in the AI Era” convened on 18 August 2026 likewise calls for AI providers to broadly disclose how their AI models are developed, including the training data involved and the methods used to collect such data.

In other words, going forward, AI solutions that cannot clearly account for how their models were developed and trained, or provide relevant information about the datasets involved, should in principle fall outside the scope of public procurement or general, large-scale deployment. Through transparency and accountability, together with an appropriate balance of rights and obligations, we can reduce the risks that may arise from the widespread adoption of AI.
